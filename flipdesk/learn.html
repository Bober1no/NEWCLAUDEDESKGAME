The central principle: a business creates value for customers and keeps enough of that value as profit to keep itself going.

1. How a business makes money

Revenue is everything you take in. It is not what you made. COGS (cost of goods sold) is the direct cost of what you sold: purchase price plus parts and repairs. Operating expenses are the costs of running the business: fuel, ads, packaging, tools.

Gross profit = Revenue − COGS
Net profit = Revenue − COGS − Operating expenses

Example: 5 PCs at $700 = $3,500 revenue. COGS $420 each = $2,100. Expenses $200. Net profit $1,200.

Markup = Profit ÷ Cost ($100 on $200 = 50%)
Margin = Profit ÷ Sale price ($100 on $300 = 33.3%)

Use margin when you compare deals. → Margin calculator

2. Cash flow: what kills profitable businesses

Spend all $3,000 on five PCs and you haven't lost money, but you can't buy anything else. If they take two months to sell, the business is frozen. That's a liquidity problem: being profitable on paper while having no cash to use.

Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Average inventory
Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Keep 15–25% of capital in cash as a reserve. Adjust it once you know how fast things actually sell. → Dashboard reserve tracker

3. Capital: how you grow

Three ways to grow your capital: put in your own money, reinvest profits, or borrow / take investment. Start by reinvesting. Split your money into four buckets: inventory, cash reserve, operating expenses, profit you take out. The cash in your account is not all money you can spend. Outstanding bills come out of it first.

Capitalₙ = Capital₀ × (1 + r)ⁿ

A 15%/month return compounds dramatically on paper. In reality, the number of good deals near you caps it. → Growth calculator

4. Pricing and valuation

There are three prices: asking (what sellers hope for), transaction (what buyers actually pay), and liquidation (what it fetches if you need cash fast). Value things on transaction prices, meaning sold listings.

MaxBuy = ExpectedSale − Repairs − SellingCosts − TargetProfit − RiskAllowance

If the asking price is over your max, walk away, even if it "looks like a good deal". That's disciplined purchasing. Always state ROI with a time period: 25% in two weeks is very different from 25% in six months. → Max buy calculator

5. Risk management

You will buy dead GPUs, bent sockets, flaky RAM, dying SSDs, fakes and liars. You can't eliminate risk, but you can price it in.

EMV = Σ (Probabilityᵢ × Outcomeᵢ)

A: 95% × $80 + 5% × −$100 = $71. B: 70% × $150 + 30% × −$100 = $75. B is slightly better on average but much more volatile. Which one is better depends on your capital and whether you can afford the bad outcome.

  • Diversify: don't put $2,500 of $3,000 into one card.
  • Sunk cost: what you already paid is gone. Decide based on the best outcome from today. → Stale-stock calculator

6. Sourcing and procurement

You make most of your profit when you buy. Sources: FB Marketplace, Gumtree, eBay, local shops, auctions (GraysOnline), office liquidations, business clearances. The edge comes from checking systematically, tracking sold prices, having fixed max-buy rules, and negotiating with data instead of emotion. → Prompt Builder: Deal hunt

7. Marketing and sales

Buyers are buying confidence, not just hardware: clear photos, exact specs, benchmarks, temperatures, honest flaws, fast and professional replies. That's how a $750 listing beats a $700 one.

Conversion = Purchases ÷ Enquiries × 100
Ad ROI = (Additional profit − Ad spend) ÷ Ad spend × 100

"Additional" matters. If it would have sold anyway, the ad didn't earn that profit. → Listing Writer

8. Operations

Make every deal repeatable: find → value → max buy → inspect → clean/repair → stress test → photograph and list → negotiate → record → reinvest. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) mean you don't rely on memory. → Checklists

9. Legal and tax in Australia

General information only, not legal or tax advice. Check the official sources, especially if you're under 18.

  • ABN: Australian Business Number. You need one once you're carrying on a business (as opposed to selling your own stuff now and then).
  • Sole trader vs company: a sole trader is you personally. A company is a separate legal entity.
  • GST: registration is generally required once GST turnover reaches $75,000. After that you lodge a BAS.
  • Income tax: business income generally has to be declared. Calling it a hobby doesn't change that. What you actually do is what counts.
  • Consumer guarantees under Australian Consumer Law can apply to second-hand goods sold by a business.
  • Second-hand dealer rules: in Victoria, buying second-hand goods to resell may require registration as a second-hand dealer. Check with the Business Licensing Authority.
  • Records: keep receipts and a ledger (the Inventory export works). Records are generally kept for 5 years.

Official sources: business.gov.au · ATO · Consumer Affairs Victoria · Victorian Business Licensing Authority

10. Strategy and competitive advantage

A competitive advantage is anything that lets you consistently do better than other flippers: better sourcing, technical skill (lower defect risk), faster testing, better presentation, reputation, automation, supplier relationships. Your technical knowledge lets you see a "worthless" office PC as a usable board, PSU and case for a budget gaming build. That's spotting value other people miss.

11. Scaling

Growth is twice the PCs for twice the hours. Scaling is twice the PCs for only slightly more hours: a standard test bench, automated price tracking, bulk buying, repeat suppliers, standard builds, batched pickups, outsourced bookkeeping. Don't scale a broken business. If every third GPU loses $50, buying 100 instead of 10 just makes the loss bigger.

Quiz

Bottom line: don't rush to get big. Get very good at making profitable buying decisions, consistently. If you can reliably turn $500 into $600, then $1,000 into $1,200, you have a repeatable business model. That matters more than having a big pile of stock.
